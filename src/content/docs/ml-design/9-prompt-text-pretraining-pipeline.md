# Prompt: LLM Text Pretraining Data Pipeline — Staff+ System Design Document

You are a Staff+ ML infrastructure engineer answering the following system design interview question, in written form, as a single self-contained HTML reference document. Write as a practitioner who has built this, not as a survey author or blogger. **Audience:** a staff-level ML systems engineer preparing for interviews — fluent in transformers, training systems, and GPU/TPU performance, but not a data-infrastructure specialist. Calibrate depth accordingly: never explain what a token is; always explain what MinHash-LSH banding actually does.

## The interview question

> Design the end-to-end data pipeline for pretraining a frontier LLM. Assume raw sources totaling **~10s of PB of compressed web crawl** (multiple CommonCrawl-scale snapshots) plus **licensed corpora, code repositories, academic/books collections, and synthetic data generation**, targeting a final training set of **~10–50T tokens** for a training run on **~10k–100k accelerators**. The pipeline must cover everything from raw WARC files landing in object storage to token streams arriving on accelerator hosts, including extraction, deduplication, filtering, decontamination, tokenization, mixture management, storage, and the online loading path. Design it as a **continuously operating system** (new crawl snapshots keep landing, filter models and recipes evolve across model generations), not a one-shot dataset build.

## Before writing: research requirement

Do a genuine survey of **current (2025–2026) industry practice** before writing. Prioritize: frontier-lab technical reports and dataset papers (open-data efforts like FineWeb/DCLM-lineage work and successors, frontier model report data sections), data-processing framework documentation actually used at scale, and papers on data-constrained scaling, mixture optimization, and synthetic data. Then, throughout the document:

- **Mark recency explicitly.** Tag claims as one of: `[established practice]` (stable for years), `[current practice 2025–26]` (what frontier teams do now, with source), or `[emerging/contested]` (proposed or partially adopted, note who claims it). Where ~2023-era practice has been superseded, say so explicitly: "this used to be done via X; current systems do Y because Z." Areas where I specifically suspect drift since 2023 and want the current state stated: heuristic-only filtering vs model-based quality classifiers; single-pass fresh-data assumptions vs deliberate multi-epoch reuse of scarce high-quality data under data-constrained scaling; uniform mixtures vs staged/annealed data schedules (mid-training and end-of-training high-quality phases); the scale and role of synthetic/rephrased data; how mixture weights are actually chosen (manual vs proxy-model experiments vs learned methods).
- Cite the source (team/paper/system name) inline for every `[current practice]` claim. If you can't find a current source, say the practice is inferred and mark it as such — do not silently present training-data-era knowledge as current. **Distinguish open-data-community practice from frontier-lab practice** where they differ: open datasets document their methods fully but often lag what frontier labs do internally; when you can only observe the open version, say so rather than presenting it as the frontier state of the art.
- **Enforcement:** every stage section must contain at least one tagged claim. Do not let tagging decay after the first sections — it applies uniformly through the final section.

## Scope: complete design, connected

Cover the **full pipeline** as one coherent system. Every section must state what it receives from the previous stage and what contract it exposes to the next (formats, granularity, throughput, metadata). Required stages, in order:

1. **Acquisition & raw storage** — source tiers (crawl / licensed / code / academic / synthetic) and their very different trust levels and legal handling; the **crawler build-vs-buy decision** (proprietary crawling vs CommonCrawl dependence: freshness, coverage, opt-out control, cost of operating a crawl fleet); object storage layout; provenance and license tagging per document; robots/opt-out compliance as a pipeline filter; **held-out eval set carving at ingest time** (why decontamination requires reserving eval material before dedup/filtering ever runs).
2. **Extraction & normalization** — WARC → text extraction (HTML boilerplate removal, and how extractor choice measurably changes downstream model quality), language identification, encoding cleanup, robustness to corrupt/adversarial documents (quarantine, retry budgets), PDF/math-specific extraction paths, and **code as its own sub-pipeline**: repo-level grouping and dedup (not file-level, and why), license filtering (permissive vs copyleft policy), code-specific quality signals (execution/lint-based filtering vs popularity heuristics).
3. **Deduplication** — the three levels and the systems behind them: exact (hashing at document and line level), near-duplicate (MinHash-LSH: explain shingling, banding, and the precision/recall knobs with numbers), and semantic (embedding-based clustering — cost of embedding 10s of billions of documents). Cross-snapshot dedup for a continuously ingesting system. The dedup-strength tradeoff: what over-deduplication destroys.
4. **Quality filtering & safety** — heuristic filters (and their current diminished role), **model-based quality classifiers as an embedded inference system**: how classifier training data is bootstrapped, fleet sizing to score tens of billions of documents, threshold selection via downstream ablations; **multilingual strategy**: why English-trained quality filters fail on other languages, per-language (or multilingual-classifier) filtering paths, and low-resource language handling; toxicity/PII handling (filter vs scrub vs keep-with-tags); the feedback loop where each model generation's filters are trained using the previous generation.
5. **Decontamination** — n-gram and fuzzy matching against the reserved eval sets, contamination auditing and reporting, why this runs after dedup/filtering but before mixing, and the arms-race problem of paraphrased contamination.
6. **Synthetic & augmented data as a generation pipeline** — rephrasing/textbook-style generation fleets, cost per synthetic token vs its measured value, quality control and collapse risks, where synthetic data sits in the mixture and how much is current practice.
7. **Tokenization & mixture management** — tokenization at scale (throughput, the backfill problem when the tokenizer changes — recompute vs lazy, with cost); **mixture/recipe design as the highest-leverage decision in the pipeline**: domain weights **and language weights**, how they are chosen (proxy-model scaling experiments, learned mixture methods), multi-epoch policy per source under data constraints, staged schedules (pretraining vs mid-training vs annealing data), **long-context data curation**: managing the document-length distribution, sourcing genuinely long documents for context-extension phases, and how length requirements interact with packing; recipe versioning and reproducibility.
8. **Training-ready storage & sharding** — tokenized shard formats and manifests, sequence packing/document boundary handling (and its attention-masking implications), shuffle strategy, storage tier and the (modest) bandwidth math — contrast explicitly with why this is trivial compared to video.
9. **Online loading path** — host-side loading, deterministic mixture sampling (proving the realized mixture matches the recipe), resumability and exact-token-position recovery under elastic restarts and reshards, epoch accounting per source, skip-and-continue semantics when bad shards are found mid-run.
10. **Lifecycle: continuous operation & versioning** — incremental processing of new crawl snapshots, **freshness management**: how recent data enters the mixture, recency weighting, and what a "knowledge cutoff" means operationally as a pipeline decision (snapshot selection, not a training-time switch); dataset snapshots as frozen manifests, backfill strategy when a filter/classifier/tokenizer changes, reproducing a months-old training run bit-for-bit.
11. **Observability & data quality in production** — per-stage yield dashboards (docs in → tokens out, with the typical retention percentages at each stage), sample-level lineage from a training token back to its source URL, detecting a data-caused loss spike or benchmark regression mid-run and rolling back.

## Cost as a first-class axis

For each major stage, give order-of-magnitude cost estimates and identify the top 2–3 cost drivers of the whole pipeline. The distinctive economics here — analyze each explicitly: (a) **filtering-classifier and synthetic-generation inference cost vs the training compute it saves or replaces** — the central "curation economics" tradeoff of text pipelines; (b) **CPU-fleet cost for extraction/dedup** at tens of PB (core-hours, not GPU-hours — show the arithmetic); (c) **yield accounting**: PB of raw crawl → % surviving each stage → final token count, as a funnel with numbers; (d) storage footprint including all derived artifacts (raw + extracted + scored + tokenized, multiplication factor over raw) and placement/colocation of CPU fleet, GPU scoring fleet, storage, and training cluster, with cross-region egress as a first-class line item. Show the arithmetic, state assumptions, use round numbers.

## Depth bar

- Every design choice must include: the alternative(s), why this one, and what breaks if the scale changes 10×. Never just name a technique — explain its mechanism in 2–4 sentences and give the number that justifies it.
- Include worked napkin math wherever a claim depends on scale (MinHash signature storage for 100B documents, classifier fleet sizing at N docs/sec, tokenization throughput, funnel yields, mixture-experiment compute budgets).
- Anticipate and answer **at least** 6 of the hardest interviewer follow-ups inline in a dedicated section. Examples of the caliber expected (do not limit yourself to these): "how do you dedup 100B documents without O(n²)," "the tokenizer changes after 60% of data is processed — walk me through the decision," "how do you know your mixture is right before spending the full training budget," "a benchmark jump is suspected to be contamination — how do you prove or disprove it," "you're data-constrained: repeat high-quality data or add lower-quality fresh data?", "a loss spike is traced to data — walk me through the investigation."
- For the **3–4 highest-leverage design decisions** in the document (your pick), append a boxed **"defend this in 60 seconds"** paragraph: the compressed spoken version of the argument, with its one key number — exactly what a candidate would say aloud at a whiteboard.

## Format

- Single self-contained HTML file, desktop-width, clean typography, no external dependencies.
- **Diagram-dense**: an SVG diagram for every stage plus one end-to-end architecture diagram drawn as a **yield funnel** — data volumes annotated on every edge (PB raw → PB extracted → % after dedup → % after filtering → final tokens). Diagrams must carry information (numbers, formats, fan-in/fan-out), not decorate.
- Tables for every comparison (extractor choices, dedup levels, filter types, shard formats, cost breakdowns, mixture recipes).
- No filler, no motivational intros, no "in conclusion" padding, no restating the question. Every paragraph must contain a decision, a number, or a mechanism.
- Length: whatever completeness requires; cut fluff, not content. **If output limits force cuts, cut in this order: prose elaboration → repeated examples → never the diagrams, tables, napkin math, follow-ups, or 60-second defenses.** Do not silently thin the later sections; mixture management, the loading path, and follow-ups matter as much as the early stages. **If any section had to be compressed below the depth bar, flag it explicitly at the top of that section** so the reader knows to regenerate it separately.
